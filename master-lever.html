The Master Lever

One box on the ballot lets a voter choose a party for every office at once. Whether it exists changes who wins, and how the winners vote.

What it is

The Master Lever, or straight-ticket voting option, lets voters select the candidates of one party for all partisan offices on the ballot with a single mark, instead of filling in each race separately. It is not a fringe habit: according to one study, between 45 and 58 percent of Texas voters used it from 1998 to 2010.

The Michigan fight of 2016

In the run-up to the 2016 general election, Michigan’s Republican-held legislature passed a bill banning the Master Lever, after several earlier attempts. Since the Great Depression the option had been thought to favour Democrats. Democrats challenged the ban, arguing that removing the quick option would disenfranchise African-American voters, and the courts let the Master Lever stay in place for November.

The outcome surprised both sides. Republicans gained from losing the case: some unexpected GOP winners received nearly half of their votes from voters who pulled the “Republican” lever.

“We ended being the benefactors of straight-ticket voting.”State Rep. Lisa Posthumus Lyons, chair of the Michigan House Elections Committee

What the research finds

Michigan is one episode in a long series of debates as most US states abolished the Master Lever over the decades. With Ioanna Grypari and Erin Hengel, I study what the option does to politicians.

The Theory of Straight Ticket VotingSocial Choice and Welfare, 2022. The option makes candidates more loyal to their party while raising the weight of non-partisan voters in candidate selection, with asymmetric effects on the two parties. One Strike and You’re Out: The Effects of the Master Lever on Senator PositionsWorking paper. In a difference-in-differences design, the Master Lever shifts US senators’ roll-call positions 3 to 6 percent to the right, driven largely by Republicans.
